# Review: Diachronic Identity — Unique Causal Lineage Generalization

**Date:** 2026-08-20  
**Reviewed investigation:** `investigations/2026-08-20-diachron-identity-causal-provenance-survivor.md`  
**Status of review:** Complete for the current generalization round  
**Framework status affected:** No canonical upgrade; UCL remains a provisional survivor and generalization remains Open.

---

## 1. Executive finding

The causal-provenance survivor has survived the original anti-circularity and counterfeit tests in an important local sense: an origin event can be specified independently of identity, and a later stage can inherit a third-person causal provenance that an internally perfect counterfeit lacks.

The remaining problem is not whether an origin can be specified. It can.

The remaining problem is whether there is a **single, principled, non-target-bearing rule for selecting the relevant origin type across domains and cases**.

The pressure tests below show a recurring fork:

1. A rule can be independently specified, but then may be too narrow, domain-specific, or ontologically arbitrary.
2. A rule can be broadened to cover the desired cases, but the broadening risks importing a selection principle whose practical role is to decide which history counts as the same entity.

UCL therefore remains a real counterexample to the broad claim that no third-person relation can distinguish a target from a perfect internal counterfeit. It is **not yet a general reductive theory of diachronic identity**.

---

## 2. What exactly has been earned

The strongest earned proposition is local:

> Given an independently specified origin predicate \(O\), a specified causal relation \(C\), and a non-branching continuation rule, the resulting lineage relation can distinguish stages with different external causal provenance even when their internal and informational states are identical.

That is enough to refute the exceptionless claim that every non-target-bearing third-person description must collapse under a perfect internal counterfeit.

It does not establish that \(O\) is universally available, uniquely determined, or metaphysically privileged for every entity type.

---

## 3. Candidate generalization A — Earliest event of a specified process type

Define an origin of type \(T\) as the earliest event \(e\) such that:

1. \(T(e)\) holds;
2. no earlier event satisfies \(T\) while standing in the specified causal relation to \(e\).

Then define identity through the maximal non-branching causal continuation of \(e\).

### Pressure

This is independently specifiable only after \(T\) is chosen.

But the choice of \(T\) does substantial work.

- For an organism, \(T\) might be fertilization or some biologically defined developmental event.
- For an artifact, \(T\) might be assembly under a construction process.
- For a process, \(T\) might be onset under some physical criterion.
- For a person, several plausible event-types compete.

The candidate therefore moves the selection problem one level down:

> Why is this process type the identity-relevant one?

That question cannot be answered by saying that it yields the desired identity verdict.

**Verdict:** independently specifiable locally; not a domain-general criterion yet.

---

## 4. Candidate generalization B — Minimal causal root

Define the relevant origin as the minimal causal root from which the later trajectory is generated.

### Ship of Theseus

A clean construction event can provide such a root.

### Duplication / fission

One root may generate two branches. The minimal-root criterion therefore does not itself distinguish the branches. The non-branching condition must terminate identity at the split.

### Convergence

Two roots may contribute to one later state. The root is no longer unique to the later state.

### Pressure

The phrase "the root from which the later trajectory is generated" can conceal a selection rule over causal relevance. Many causal ancestors may be necessary or contributory. Choosing the "minimal" one requires a principled ordering over causes.

Without such an ordering, the candidate is underdefined. With an ordering tailored to identity cases, it risks becoming target-bearing.

**Verdict:** does not yet solve the origin-selection problem.

---

## 5. Candidate generalization C — Unique causal ancestor under a complete causal graph

A stronger attempt is:

> The relevant origin is the unique ancestor satisfying an independently specified causal characterization, and the identity relation follows the unique maximal non-branching path from it.

This is close to the current UCL formulation.

### Pressure

It works whenever the graph actually contains one uniquely characterized ancestor.

But uniqueness is now a condition on the world, not something the criterion guarantees. Many entities have multiple causally relevant predecessors, interacting causes, mergers, or shared production histories.

If the rule says that identity exists only where such uniqueness obtains, that is coherent but highly revisionary: many ordinary persistence cases become identity-indeterminate or identity-terminating.

If the rule is expanded to choose among multiple origins, the selection rule becomes the new pressure point.

**Verdict:** coherent restricted theory; insufficient as a universal theory without additional origin-selection principles.

---

## 6. Candidate generalization D — Counterfactual causal indispensability

A tempting rescue is to define the relevant origin as the event such that:

> If that event had not occurred, the later stage would not have existed in the relevant form.

This is independently stated and does not contain the word identity.

### Pressure: overdetermination

Multiple causes can each be counterfactually indispensable under different intervention models, or none may be uniquely indispensable when redundant causes exist.

### Pressure: fission

The predecessor event can remain counterfactually necessary for both branches. Counterfactual indispensability therefore does not select one successor over another.

### Pressure: reconstruction

An original construction event may be indispensable to a history even when the later object has been disassembled and reconstructed. Whether that establishes identity or merely causal dependence remains unresolved.

### Pressure: intervention dependence

The result of a counterfactual depends on which variables are held fixed and which interventions are considered admissible. A choice of intervention model that selects the desired origin risks becoming a hidden selection principle.

**Verdict:** useful causal relation, but not yet identity criterion.

---

## 7. Candidate generalization E — Maximal non-branching causal lineage alone

Drop unique origin entirely and define identity by membership in the same maximal non-branching causal segment.

### Pressure

This appears attractive because it removes the origin-selection problem.

But it loses the very asymmetry that made UCL beat the internal counterfeit. A counterfeit can be given its own maximal non-branching causal segment with identical internal structure.

Furthermore, a process can have several equally legitimate maximal segments depending on how branch and merge points are treated.

Most importantly, without a unique external anchor, the criterion again needs something beyond internal structure to distinguish provenance.

**Verdict:** fails to preserve the survivor's decisive feature.

---

## 8. Equivalence-relation pressure

A genuine theory of diachronic identity should define a coherent identity relation, not merely a persistence heuristic.

Suppose UCL-relatedness is defined by membership in one unique-origin, non-branching lineage segment.

### Reflexivity

A stage should belong to its own lineage. This is straightforward if lineage segments are well-defined.

### Symmetry

If two stages belong to the same lineage segment, the relation is symmetric.

### Transitivity

If stages \(A\) and \(B\) lie on one segment, and \(B\) and \(C\) lie on one segment, then all three must lie on the same segment. This holds if the segmentation is genuinely a partition of the stages.

### The unresolved point

Branch and merge events complicate the partition. A principled rule is needed for whether the branching/merging event itself belongs to a predecessor segment, a successor segment, a new segment, or multiple segments.

This is not cosmetic. It determines whether the candidate produces a genuine equivalence relation or only a path-based heuristic.

**Verdict:** equivalence structure is available in simple trees; general graphs require an explicit segmentation rule.

---

## 9. Ship of Theseus under generalization

UCL continues to perform well on gradual replacement provided the causal lineage remains unique and non-branching.

But this result is less informative than it initially appeared: almost any historical-continuity account can handle gradual replacement.

The decisive Theseus case is therefore the reconstruction fork:

- continuous successor using replaced components;
- later vessel reconstructed from the original components.

UCL chooses the continuous successor because provenance differs.

That is coherent.

The deeper question remains whether causal provenance is an identity-maker or simply a well-defined historical relation that correlates with ordinary identity in this case.

The candidate must not silently infer the former from the latter.

---

## 10. Duplication and fission

The non-branching clause gives UCL a clean answer:

\[
A\rightarrow\{B,C\}
\]

terminates the pre-branch identity relation.

That avoids the numerical-identity contradiction of making both \(B\) and \(C\) identical to \(A\).

But the price is substantial and explicit:

> **fission destroys diachronic identity at the split.**

This is not a failure, because disagreement with an intuition is not a logical contradiction. It is a substantive commitment that future evidence or conceptual pressure could still challenge.

A future theory that preserves identity through fission would therefore be a direct counterexample to the non-branching clause, not merely a variant of UCL.

---

## 11. Convergence and fusion

For:

\[
A\rightarrow C\leftarrow B,
\]

UCL cannot preserve identity from both \(A\) and \(B\) into \(C\) without violating the uniqueness of lineage.

This gives a coherent termination rule, but again only because the theory treats fusion as an identity-breaking event.

Thus the candidate is increasingly best described as a theory of **exclusive historical persistence** rather than yet a general theory of personal identity.

That wording should not be treated as pejorative; it is a scope clarification. The theory earns one kind of persistence relation very well, but it has not yet shown that ordinary identity is reducible to that relation.

---

## 12. The central generalization problem

The decisive pressure can now be stated without examples:

> **What makes an event an identity-relevant origin rather than merely a causally important event?**

Three possible answers all face problems:

1. **Object/organism type:** may work locally but requires a principled account of which type matters.
2. **Physical causal structure:** may provide unique roots in some systems but not all, and may be underdetermined by multiple causal parents.
3. **Counterfactual necessity:** depends on an intervention model and can identify several causes or none.

No fourth general rule has yet been supplied.

The important methodological result is therefore not “origin fails.” It is:

\[
\boxed{\text{origin can be specified independently locally, but general identity-relevance of origin remains unearned.}}
\]

---

## 13. Status update

### Earned

- Local independent origin predicates exist.
- UCL breaks the perfect internal-counterfeit construction when the counterfeit differs in external causal provenance.
- UCL survives the current Ship-of-Theseus, duplication, fission, convergence, discontinuity, memory-loss, and reconstruction tests under its explicit assumptions.

### Open

- A domain-general, non-arbitrary origin-selection rule.
- Whether the resulting lineage relation is a principled equivalence relation on general causal structures.
- Whether the fission/merger termination behavior is an acceptable identity result or merely a consequence of the chosen persistence criterion.
- Whether “causal provenance” explains identity or merely supplies a distinct historical relation that happens to track identity in the tested cases.

### Not earned

- That UCL is the correct theory of personal identity.
- That every person/entity has one uniquely relevant origin.
- That the Exceptional Zero explains UCL or diachronic identity.
- That the recurring signature is restored by abandoning internal relations.

---

## 14. Recommended next test

Do not generate more origin rules immediately.

The next move should be to **formalize the selection problem itself**.

Define a candidate class of origin predicates \(\mathcal O\) and ask:

> What properties must an origin predicate satisfy to be independent of identity, stable under isomorphic redescription, and sufficient to generate a non-branching lineage relation?

Then test the candidate axioms against:

- multiple causal parents;
- overdetermination;
- shared production events;
- cloning;
- twinning;
- fusion;
- reconstruction;
- branching processes;
- temporally separated continuation.

If no plausible axiomatization survives, UCL may settle into a **Boundary for reduction to causal provenance** rather than merely remaining an open candidate.

For now, however, that stronger status is not earned.
